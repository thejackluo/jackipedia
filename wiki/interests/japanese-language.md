## The JLPT Scale

The Japanese Language Proficiency Test (JLPT) has five levels:

| Level | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| N5 | Basic — can understand simple Japanese |
| N4 | Elementary — can understand everyday Japanese |
| N3 | Intermediate — can understand Japanese to a degree |
| N2 | Pre-advanced — can understand Japanese used in everyday situations |
| N1 | Advanced — can understand Japanese in a broad range of situations |

Being between N3 and N4 means Jack can read hiragana and katakana fluently, has a working vocabulary of 1,000–2,000 words, understands basic grammatical structures, and can handle simple everyday conversations. He cannot yet handle native-speed conversations, complex kanji, or formal written Japanese.

This is several hundred hours of study, minimum.

## Learning Japanese

Japanese is notoriously difficult for English speakers. The US Foreign Service Institute classifies it as a Category IV language (the hardest category), estimating 2,200 classroom hours to reach professional proficiency. The three writing systems — hiragana (syllabary), katakana (syllabary for foreign words), and kanji (Chinese-derived logographic characters, 2,000+ needed for basic literacy) — are the main challenge.

At N3-N4 level, Jack has cleared the hiragana/katakana hurdle and has a working set of kanji. The path to N2 and N1 is primarily about kanji accumulation, vocabulary expansion, and immersion.

## Classical Chinese and Japanese

The Pulleyblank book (Outline of Classical Chinese Grammar) and Japanese are both in active study. This is unusual — most people learning an East Asian language don't simultaneously study classical versions of another. But the overlap is real: classical Chinese is the literary and scholarly ancestor of both modern Chinese and significant portions of the Japanese writing system. Many kanji are shared between the two languages (though often with different readings and sometimes different meanings).
